Can You Brush Your Teeth with Tap Water Abroad?

In countries rated Safe or Generally Safe, brushing your teeth with tap water carries essentially no risk. In Caution-rated countries, brushing with the tap is a small, usually acceptable risk for healthy adults if you spit thoroughly. Only in Not Safe destinations is bottled water for brushing genuinely worth the hassle.

Why Brushing Is Lower Risk Than Drinking

Infection risk from contaminated water scales with dose: the more pathogen-laden water you swallow, the more likely enough organisms survive stomach acid to establish an infection. Brushing your teeth involves wetting a brush, scrubbing, and spitting — a careful adult swallows at most a milliliter or two, versus 250 ml in a glass of water. That's a hundred-fold difference in exposure.

Lower dose is not zero dose, though. Some pathogens have very small infectious doses — Shigella and norovirus can infect with just a handful of organisms, and Giardia cysts with as few as ten. In heavily contaminated water, even brushing volumes matter. That's why the advice scales with how bad the water actually is, rather than being a single yes or no.

Match the Precaution to the Destination Rating

  • Safe / Generally Safe (most of Europe, Japan, Australia, North America north of the Rio Grande): brush from the tap without a second thought. Bottled water here is pure overcaution.
  • Caution (much of Latin America and urban Southeast Asia, e.g. Thailand): healthy adults who rinse and spit thoroughly are very unlikely to get sick from brushing. Using bottled water is a reasonable belt-and-suspenders choice, especially early in a trip before your routine settles.
  • Not Safe (e.g. India, much of Sub-Saharan Africa): use bottled or purified water for brushing. Water quality here can be poor enough that even small ingested volumes carry meaningful risk, and travelers routinely trace illness to exactly this habit.

Check your destination on the world safety map before you go — the rating tells you which row you're in.

The Bottled Water Technique

Where bottled water is warranted, the mechanics are simple but benefit from a routine. Keep a dedicated bottle by the sink — that placement is the whole trick, because the dangerous moment is the half-asleep, muscle-memory rinse under the running tap. Wet the brush from the bottle, brush normally, sip from the bottle to rinse, and spit. A 500 ml bottle lasts several days of brushing.

Two habits close the remaining gaps: keep your mouth closed in the shower (shower water is the same tap water), and dry the brush head standing up rather than resting it on the sink rim. Some travelers rinse the brush in bottled water after use as well; that's sensible in Not Safe destinations, ritualistic anywhere else.

Who Should Be More Careful

The healthy-adult calculus doesn't apply to everyone. Small children swallow far more while brushing — often deliberately — so in Caution countries, kids should get bottled water at the sink even when their parents don't bother. The same escalation applies to pregnant travelers, the elderly, and anyone immunocompromised or on stomach-acid-reducing medication (proton pump inhibitors reduce your stomach's pathogen-killing ability, a genuinely underappreciated risk factor for travelers).

If anyone in your group is in these categories, simply treat the destination as one rating worse than listed and act accordingly.

Overcaution Has Costs Too

It's worth saying plainly: brushing with bottled water in Paris, Tokyo, or Copenhagen accomplishes nothing except plastic waste and expense. Travel health advice tends to ratchet toward maximum caution because nobody gets blamed for being too careful — but a rule applied where it isn't needed makes people likelier to abandon it where it is. Save the vigilance for destinations that need it, and spend it fully there.

The honest summary: this precaution matters a great deal in a small set of countries, a little in a middle band, and not at all in most of the developed world. Knowing which band you're in is the entire game — which is what the country pages on this site are for.
