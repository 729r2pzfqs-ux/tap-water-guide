Should You Drink Hotel Tap Water? A Traveler's Guide

Hotel tap water starts as the municipal supply — so the destination's rating on our map is your baseline — but the building adds its own variables: storage tanks, aging pipes, and in some regions rooftop cisterns that sit warm for years. In Safe-rated countries, drink from the hotel tap freely; in Caution and Not Safe countries, the hotel's plumbing is one more reason to stick with sealed or purified water.

A Hotel Is a Small Water System of Its Own

Water arrives at a hotel from the city main, then passes through the building's own infrastructure: ground or rooftop storage tanks, pumps, pressure systems, heaters, and hundreds of meters of internal pipework. In a modern hotel in a Safe-rated country, this adds essentially nothing to your risk — the water at the room tap is the same water the city delivers, and city water in Japan, Western Europe, or North America is dependable.

In older buildings and hotter climates, the building layer matters more. Storage tanks can accumulate sediment and biofilm if poorly maintained; long-unused wings can hold stagnant water; and legacy plumbing can contribute metals. The useful mental model: hotel tap safety = city supply quality + building storage and plumbing, and you can assess the first factor precisely before you arrive via the country and city pages on this site.

Rooftop Tanks: The Region-Specific Wildcard

Across the Middle East, South Asia, Latin America, and much of Africa, buildings commonly store water in rooftop tanks to buffer intermittent municipal pressure. The design is sensible; the maintenance is the issue. Tanks that sit at 30–40°C, with imperfect covers and infrequent cleaning, are a growth medium — and they're the standard explanation for why water that left a modern treatment or desalination plant in good shape (see our desalination guide) is nonetheless not drunk from the tap by locals.

Travelers can't inspect the roof, so use the proxy signals: in countries where rooftop storage is the norm and the country page recommends bottled water, apply that advice inside hotels too, including high-end ones. An international five-star may maintain excellent filtration; you simply can't verify it from the lobby, and the sealed bottle costs a dollar.

The Kettle Question

The in-room kettle is the traveler's purification plant: a full rolling boil makes microbiologically suspect water safe to drink (one minute — details in our purification guide), which covers tea, coffee, and instant meals even in Not Safe destinations. Two sensible practices: run one full boil-and-discard cycle first (kettles get used for questionable purposes — a documented if overhyped traveler phenomenon — and may hold stale water), and fill from the cold tap, since hot-side water has been sitting in the building's heater.

Note the limits: boiling handles microbes, not chemical contamination, and a kettle's convenience shouldn't lure you into drinking unboiled tap water in a bottled-water destination just because the room provides glassware. Which brings us to the glasses.

Glasses, Ice, and Minibar Details

Room glassware is only as clean as housekeeping's last shift — investigations in several countries have caught glasses being wiped with the same cloth used elsewhere in the room rather than washed. In destinations where water safety already demands care, rinse glasses with bottled or boiled water before use, or drink from the bottle. Wrapped disposable cups are the trustworthy option when provided.

Hotel ice deserves the same reasoning as our ice guide lays out: in reputable hotels, ice machines are typically fed by filtered or purified water even in Caution countries, and asking the front desk “is the ice made from purified water?” is routine. Minibar bottled water is safe but marked up several hundred percent; the corner shop sells the same sealed bottle for a fraction. Check seals wherever you buy.

A Simple Decision Framework

  • Safe countries: drink the room tap. Refill your bottle at the sink. This includes essentially all hotels in Western Europe, North America, Japan, Australia, and New Zealand.
  • Generally Safe countries: the tap is normally fine in modern hotels; if the building is visibly old or the water tastes off, default to bottled and mention it to the front desk — they'll know the building's situation.
  • Caution countries: bottled or purified water for drinking and consider it for brushing; kettle-boiled tap water for hot drinks; ask about ice and filtered-water stations. Many hotels in these countries provide free daily bottles — use them.
  • Not Safe countries: sealed bottled or purifier-treated water for everything that enters your mouth, including brushing; boil-and-discard the kettle before use; rinse glasses with safe water. A purifier bottle (see our filter guide) turns the bathroom tap into your supply and pays for itself within days.

Look up your destination on the world safety map before arrival — the country page tells you which row applies, and city pages add local specifics like Mexico's building-storage caveats.
